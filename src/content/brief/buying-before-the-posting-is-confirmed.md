> **For information only.** This article is an editorial orientation resource. It does not constitute legal, tax or customs advice. Vehicle exemption procedures vary by country and change periodically. Always confirm the current position with your mission's administrative office and the relevant customs or transport authority before purchasing a vehicle.

---

The instinct is understandable. A posting is all but certain, the paperwork is moving, and buying the car early means one less thing to arrange after arrival. In several of the countries covered on this desk, that instinct creates a real risk, because the vehicle exemption is not attached to the fact of moving. It is attached to the accreditation itself, confirmed and on file with a specific authority, before the purchase.

In France, the customs relief on a diplomatically-registered vehicle runs from the date the DGDDI issues the franchise authorisation, not from the date of purchase. A vehicle bought before that authorisation exists is bought as an ordinary private purchase, at full VAT, with no mechanism to retroactively apply the diplomatic exemption once accreditation comes through. The sequence has to run in the correct order: accreditation confirmed, franchise authorisation issued, then the purchase.

Singapore's position is stricter still, because the exemption there is not just a tax relief but access to a vehicle registration system with a genuinely different cost structure. The Certificate of Entitlement exemption for accredited diplomatic and consular staff, granted under the Road Traffic (Diplomatic and Consular Privileges) (Exemption) Order 2006, applies to those already holding confirmed accreditation. A vehicle bought before that status is confirmed enters the ordinary COE bidding system, where a Category A certificate alone can run over SGD 100,000. There is no route to reclaim that difference after the fact.

Austria's MA46 process for the WD plate series follows the same logic through a different mechanism. The vehicle registration and its associated VAT and tax treatment are processed once the Federal Ministry has confirmed the accreditation to the Vienna municipal transport authority, not before.

**The pattern across all three is the same.** The exemption is not a feature of being a diplomat in general. It is a feature of a specific accreditation record existing with a specific authority at the moment of purchase. Buying ahead of that record does not save time. It usually means paying the full domestic price for a vehicle and then separately navigating the diplomatic registration process for a different vehicle once accredited, or accepting that the one already purchased will never carry the exemption.

There is a narrower and more defensible version of buying early: ordering a vehicle for delivery timed to arrive after accreditation is expected to be confirmed, with the dealer or your mission's administrative office aware that the registration will need to wait for that confirmation. This is different from completing a private purchase and registration before the accreditation exists. The distinction is not always obvious to a dealer unfamiliar with diplomatic sales, which is another reason to involve your mission's administrative office in the timing conversation rather than treating the vehicle purchase as a personal errand to get out of the way early.

The honest rule of thumb: if there is any uncertainty about when accreditation will be confirmed, the vehicle decision should wait for that confirmation, not anticipate it.
