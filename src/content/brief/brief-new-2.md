---

The titre de séjour spécial in France, the legitimation card from the FDFA in Geneva, the special identity card from the Belgian Protocol Directorate: none of these arrive on the day you land. The notification process takes time, the cards are issued in batches, and the gap between arrival and receiving the document can easily run to four to eight weeks. In that interval, you are legally present and your status is being processed, but you have nothing concrete to show for it.

Most experienced administrative officers deal with this routinely. Most newly arrived families discover it is a problem when they try to open a bank account on day three.

**What the gap looks like in practice**

Banks are the sharpest friction point. French, Swiss and Belgian banks generally require the residence document before opening a personal account. An attestation letter from your mission, on letterhead, confirming your accreditation and anticipated arrival date of the card can sometimes substitute, but not always and not at every branch. Some missions have pre-existing relationships with specific banks or branches accustomed to this situation. Ask your administrative office before arrival which bank and which branch to approach.

Car registration cannot proceed until the card is in hand. In France, the MEAE requires the titre de séjour spécial before processing the vehicle registration request with the DGDDI. If you are arriving with a vehicle or planning to purchase one, build the card delay into your timeline. A vehicle sitting unregistered for six weeks is a manageable inconvenience; a vehicle purchased before the card arrives and needed for a school run is a more urgent problem.

School enrolment is less affected than people expect. Most international schools accept a mission attestation letter in place of a residence document for the initial enrolment. The card is then provided when it arrives. Confirm this directly with the school's admissions office before arrival.

Health coverage is the most critical gap to close before you arrive, not after. The carte de légitimation or titre de séjour spécial does not grant access to the host state's national health system in any case. Your coverage must come from your home state scheme, your organisation's staff health insurance, or a private arrangement. The gap period is precisely when disrupted routines, travel fatigue and unfamiliar environments make healthcare access most likely to be needed. Confirm your coverage is active and portable before you board the plane.

**The interim document**

Most missions and international organisations have a standard procedure for the gap. In France, the MEAE can provide a temporary attestation to the mission confirming the notification has been submitted. In Geneva, the FDFA section issues a similar document. In Brussels, the Protocol Directorate has equivalent interim procedures. These documents are not the card, but they carry official weight and are generally accepted by banks, schools and landlords familiar with the diplomatic community.

The key action is simple: ask your mission's administrative office, before you arrive, what document they provide in the interim and how long it typically takes from notification to card issuance. The answer tells you how long to plan for the gap and what you can and cannot do during it.
